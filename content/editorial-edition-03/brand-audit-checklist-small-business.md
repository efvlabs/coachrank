An independent business can look polished and still be difficult to understand. The homepage describes one service, a social profile mentions another, and the link a potential customer follows leads to a booking page with no explanation of the meeting.

A useful brand audit starts by following that journey. It gives you a list of observable problems and a reason to fix one before the others. It does not need to begin with a new visual identity.

This checklist is designed for a small business, consultant or personal brand selling a specific offer. Set aside a first session to review one buying journey. A complex business with several products, markets or locations will need a broader investigation.

## What is a brand audit?

A brand audit is a structured review of how a business presents itself and how that presentation compares with customer expectations and experience. This guide focuses on a practical first pass: audience, offer, message, evidence and the path to taking action.

It is narrower than a full research engagement. It cannot establish what an entire market thinks, measure brand value or prove why sales are low. It can reveal contradictions, unanswered questions and broken steps worth investigating.

For the strategic starting point, read [Brand Clarity Before Brand Identity](https://coachrank.lol/blog/brand-clarity-before-brand-identity). For this audit, keep a record of what you can actually observe.

## Choose one customer journey

Write a sentence naming the offer and the person you are reviewing it for. For example: “A newly independent consultant discovers my website from a LinkedIn post and considers a service-page redesign.”

Collect the pages that person could encounter:

- The social profile or search result that introduces the business.
- The homepage or article they land on.
- The relevant service or product page.
- A sample, portfolio or explanation of the work.
- The enquiry, booking or checkout step.
- The confirmation and delivery instructions you control.

Open the journey on your phone as well as a larger screen. If you test a form, use your own system and a clearly identified test submission. Use the payment provider's test environment or an authorized purchase for payment verification. Reaching a checkout page does not establish that payment and delivery work.

## The small-business brand audit checklist

For every item, record **clear**, **needs work** or **not checked**. Add the page, screenshot or customer observation behind the decision. Avoid adding up ticks into an unsupported brand-health score.

### 1. Can someone recognize who the offer serves?

Look for a customer situation, not only a broad label such as “ambitious people” or “growing businesses.” A visitor should be able to recognize when the service would become relevant.

**Check:** Does the opening identify the intended customer or their problem? Does the example match that audience? Does the page explain any meaningful limits on fit?

**Useful evidence:** The actual opening copy and a reader's explanation of who it serves.

**Possible fix:** Replace a broad introduction with a specific situation. Keep wider background information elsewhere if it still helps the buyer.

### 2. Is the exchange understandable?

A promise of clarity, growth or confidence does not explain the purchase. Find the deliverable, format, scope, time requirement and price or quote process.

**Check:** Can a visitor distinguish a course from a consultation, an assessment from an independent audit, and a download from an ongoing service? Can they tell what they must provide?

**Useful evidence:** The product description, sample and checkout wording agree about the same purchase.

**Possible fix:** Add a short “What you receive” section. For services, state dependencies and revision limits. See our guide to [defining service scope](https://coachrank.lol/blog/scope-your-service-before-setting-the-price).

### 3. Does the message survive a change of channel?

Read the homepage, social bio, offer page and booking description together. Different lengths are fine. Contradictory offers are a problem.

**Check:** Are the audience, service name, price and main promise consistent? Does an old profile send people to an offer you no longer provide? Does the linked article explain the next step accurately?

**Useful evidence:** A small table of the exact claims on each page, dated when you checked them.

**Possible fix:** Choose one current description of the offer and update the outdated references. Use the [positioning statement exercise](https://coachrank.lol/blog/brand-positioning-statement-examples) if the underlying decision is still unclear.

### 4. Is there a reason to choose this approach?

Compare the offer with what the customer would otherwise do. That may be another provider, an existing tool, doing the work themselves or waiting.

**Check:** Is the difference concrete? Could you explain it without “premium,” “innovative” or “passionate”? Does it involve a real tradeoff, such as a narrow specialization or a bounded delivery process?

**Useful evidence:** A sample or demonstration that makes the difference visible.

**Possible fix:** Explain one relevant difference and its boundary. The [SBA's competitive-analysis guidance](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/plan-your-business/#market-research-and-competitive-analysis) can help you examine alternatives; do not assume every business in your category competes for the same decision.

### 5. Can people inspect the evidence behind your claims?

List the strongest statements on the page and put their supporting evidence beside them. Check what each example actually demonstrates.

**Check:** Are testimonials used with permission? Are samples clearly labeled? Can you distinguish your work from a client's work? Do qualifications and results say what they mean without exaggeration?

**Useful evidence:** A relevant sample, a documented process, a verifiable qualification or a permissioned account of completed work.

**Possible fix:** Narrow an unsupported claim or provide appropriate evidence. If you are new, show a clearly labeled demonstration. A fictional example can explain your method, but cannot serve as a customer success story.

### 6. Does the next step match its description?

Follow the main action from the offer page. Note what you expected and what appeared. A button labeled “View sample” should reveal a sample rather than an unexpected sales call form.

**Check:** Do the links work? Is the form usable on mobile? Is the price and payment frequency clear before commitment? Does the confirmation explain what happens next and how to get help?

**Useful evidence:** The URL, visible screen and outcome at each step. Mark untested payment or delivery steps as not checked.

**Possible fix:** Repair a broken action, clarify a label or explain an unexpected step. Our [customer onboarding guide](https://coachrank.lol/blog/customer-onboarding-first-useful-result) covers the journey to the first useful result.

## Decide what to fix first

Use three questions to sort your findings:

1. Does this prevent someone from completing an intended action?
2. Could it materially mislead them about the offer or commitment?
3. Can you explain the confusion with observed evidence?

A broken enquiry link or incorrect price deserves prompt attention. A repeated misunderstanding deserves a focused revision and another check. A personal preference for a different color can wait unless there is a concrete usability issue.

This is a practical order for investigation, not a prediction of revenue impact. Several problems can exist at once, and some changes require customer research before implementation.

## A worked example: an independent consultant

Imagine a fictional consultant whose homepage promises a “complete business transformation.” The offer page sells a two-session review. The social bio still links to a retired booking page.

The audit might look like this:

| Finding | Evidence | Next action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Booking route fails | The profile link opens an unavailable page | Replace the link and test the new destination |
| Scope is inconsistent | Homepage and offer page describe different levels of work | Explain the two-session review consistently |
| Deliverable is hard to judge | The offer mentions a report without showing its contents | Publish a clearly labeled sample with any private details removed |
| Buyer understanding is unknown | No intended customers have reviewed the revised page | Ask a few suitable readers to explain the offer back |

The sequence produces a repaired journey and clearer material to test. It does not prove that those changes will produce sales. Keep both conclusions separate.

## Copy this audit record

Use one row for each issue. This is the complete worksheet; no purchase is required.

| Field | Your note |
| --- | --- |
| Offer and intended customer | What journey are you reviewing? |
| Location and date | Which page or step did you inspect, and when? |
| Finding | What happened or what was unclear? |
| Evidence | Save the wording, screenshot or reader observation |
| Consequence | What decision or action could this affect? |
| Proposed change | Name one bounded improvement |
| Owner and review point | Who will act, and when will you check again? |
| Result | Record what changed and what remains unknown |

Revisit the record after a meaningful change to the offer, after repeated customer confusion or before a promotion that brings new visitors. You do not need to repeat every check whenever you edit a sentence.
