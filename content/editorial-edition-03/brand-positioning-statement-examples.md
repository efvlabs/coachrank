A positioning statement becomes useful when it helps you make a choice. It should tell you which customer to write for, which problem to lead with, and which promises your business can actually support.

That is a different job from sounding impressive. A consultant who offers “innovative solutions for ambitious businesses” has left almost every decision open. A consultant who helps small agencies replace inconsistent project handovers has given their website, service and next customer conversation a direction.

This guide gives you a working statement, six illustrative examples and a way to test the result. You can complete the exercise in a document. You do not need a new logo or a paid tool to begin.

## What is a brand positioning statement?

A brand positioning statement describes the customer you serve, the situation in which your offer matters, the kind of solution you provide, and a relevant reason to choose it over alternatives. It is a working decision for the business. Your public headline can be shorter, but it should express the same underlying offer.

A tagline might make a brand memorable. A value proposition explains the value of an offer. A positioning statement adds the competitive context: who it suits and how it compares with the options that person already has.

[Strategyzer's Value Proposition Canvas](https://www.strategyzer.com/library/the-value-proposition-canvas) is a useful companion for examining a customer's work, difficulties and desired outcomes. Use that research to inform your positioning. Filling in a sentence cannot establish that people want the offer.

## Start with the decision your customer faces

Write down a recent situation in which someone sought your help. What happened just before they started looking? What were they using or doing already? What made that approach difficult to continue?

If you have no customers yet, label these answers as assumptions. Talk to people who encounter the problem and ask about a specific recent experience. Our guide to [customer interviews](https://coachrank.lol/blog/customer-interviews-without-selling-the-answer) explains how to investigate without turning the conversation into a pitch.

The [U.S. Small Business Administration's market research guidance](https://www.sba.gov/counseling/plan-your-business/#market-research-and-competitive-analysis) distinguishes direct research from existing information and recommends examining alternatives. For this exercise, include doing the work internally, using a spreadsheet, hiring someone else and postponing the purchase. A competitor is useful context only if your customer would actually consider it.

## A positioning statement template you can adapt

Use this original CoachRank worksheet as a first draft:

> When [specific customer] needs to [job or change], [offer] provides [concrete help]. Compared with [current alternative], it offers [relevant difference]. We can support that difference with [evidence]. It is a poor fit when [boundary].

The final two sentences are deliberately demanding. They help you distinguish a difference you can demonstrate from an attractive adjective.

You do not have to put this entire paragraph on your homepage. Keep it beside your draft and use it to decide what belongs there. If you cannot complete a field honestly, leave a question in its place rather than manufacture a claim.

## Six examples for solo businesses

The businesses below are fictional. Their statements demonstrate choices, not proven market demand or actual client results.

### 1. A leadership coach

**Starting point:** “I empower leaders to unlock their potential.”

**Working position:** “I help newly promoted managers prepare for difficult team conversations through six individual coaching sessions and practice between meetings. The service suits managers who want to examine their own approach; it does not provide an outsourced HR function.”

The customer situation and service are recognizable. Before publishing, the coach should explain relevant experience and training, and describe the working process. The statement avoids promising a promotion or a specific team outcome.

### 2. An operations consultant

**Starting point:** “I streamline businesses for scalable success.”

**Working position:** “I help small design agencies document the handover from a signed proposal to project delivery. A two-week engagement produces a handover checklist, responsibility map and walkthrough using the agency's existing tools.”

The difference is a bounded implementation rather than a broad transformation project. A sample checklist can make the deliverable easier to evaluate. The consultant still needs to state what information and staff access the work requires.

### 3. An independent web designer

**Starting point:** “Beautiful websites that convert.”

**Working position:** “I design service websites for independent consultants whose offers have outgrown their current pages. We organize the service, evidence and enquiry path before building in the client's existing website platform.”

This explains why the buyer might act now and how the work proceeds. An annotated portfolio can support the process claim. Conversion improvements would need separate evidence; a good design process alone cannot establish them.

### 4. A freelance writer

**Starting point:** “Compelling content for every brand.”

**Working position:** “I turn a software company's completed customer projects into case-study drafts through an interview, evidence review and client approval process. Each engagement covers one story and an agreed set of revisions.”

The buyer can imagine both the input and the finished work. The evidence might be a permissioned sample with interview notes and an explanation of editorial decisions. Confidential projects require an appropriate approval process before publication.

### 5. A solo software founder

**Starting point:** “The all-in-one productivity platform.”

**Working position:** “Our tool gives freelance video editors one place to collect timestamped client feedback on a draft. It is designed for a small review group that currently mixes email threads and chat messages, with no editing-suite migration required.”

The meaningful comparison is with the current review process. A working demonstration can support the claim about feedback collection. Claims about compatibility, permissions and file limits should match the actual product.

### 6. An independent workshop facilitator

**Starting point:** “Transformative learning experiences.”

**Working position:** “I run a half-day workshop for small remote teams that need a shared way to document decisions. Participants leave with a decision-note template and an agreement about where it will be used and reviewed.”

The deliverable is a usable working agreement. A sample agenda and anonymized demonstration can help a buyer judge fit. The facilitator should not treat attendance as proof of long-term behavior change.

## Turn your statement into website copy

Separate the internal decision from the public explanation. For the fictional operations consultant, the website opening could read:

> A clearer handover for your next agency project.
>
> Document what happens between a signed proposal and delivery. In two weeks, we build your handover checklist and responsibility map using the tools your team already has.
>
> See the engagement outline.

The supporting page would explain scope, price or quote process, dependencies and examples. The call to action should lead to the engagement outline it promises.

Avoid forcing every detail into the headline. Give each part of the page a job. Our guide to [writing an offer page](https://coachrank.lol/blog/offer-page-that-answers-the-buyers-questions) covers the information a buyer needs to make the next decision.

## Test understanding before polishing the language

Show the draft to a few people who resemble the intended customer. Ask them to read it, then explain what they think you provide without looking back.

Ask these questions without coaching their answers:

1. Who does this seem to be for?
2. What would you expect to receive?
3. What situation would make this relevant?
4. What would you compare it with?
5. What would you need to know before taking the next step?

Record their words. “This sounds professional” gives you less to work with than “I thought you would run our projects for us.” Revise the most consequential misunderstanding and repeat with fresh readers when possible.

A handful of conversations can expose confusion. It cannot establish a conversion rate, prove demand or represent your whole market. Treat the exercise as a way to improve the next draft and the next research question.

## Your one-page positioning worksheet

Copy these prompts into a document and answer each in one or two sentences:

| Prompt | What to write |
| --- | --- |
| Customer and trigger | Who needs help, and what has just happened? |
| Current alternative | What do they do without your offer? |
| Concrete exchange | What do they receive, and what must they contribute? |
| Useful difference | Which part would change their choice? |
| Available evidence | What can a stranger inspect today? |
| Boundary | When should they choose something else? |
| Next test | Which assumption will you investigate first? |

Keep the completed sheet with your website draft. Revisit it when the offer changes or customer evidence challenges your assumptions.
