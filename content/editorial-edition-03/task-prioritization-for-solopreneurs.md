When you work alone, the same person writes the proposal, answers support questions, fixes the website and decides what the business should become. Every task can make a plausible case for attention.

The practical difficulty is choosing a week that fits. A list of important tasks does not tell you whether the work is possible in the hours you have or whether one task is blocking all the others.

This method starts with commitments and capacity, then compares a small number of optional tasks. It ends with a plan you can put on a calendar. A notebook or ordinary document is enough.

## How should a solopreneur prioritize tasks?

Protect existing obligations, identify one near-term business outcome, and estimate the time you can actually use. Compare the remaining tasks by their connection to that outcome, available evidence, dependencies and effort. Choose a small set that fits, leave room for uncertainty, and give deferred work a review condition.

This is an original CoachRank planning exercise. It is not an optimized scheduling algorithm or a guarantee of business results. Its purpose is to make the tradeoffs visible enough to choose and review.

## Reserve time for commitments before ranking opportunities

Begin with the work you have already promised. Include client delivery, essential maintenance, scheduled appointments and any administrative deadlines that apply to your business.

Record each commitment's due date, consequence of delay and remaining effort. If the commitments already exceed your capacity, the next task is to renegotiate, narrow or get help. Moving them below an exciting project in a priority list does not remove the obligation.

Separate these from preferences. A launch date you chose yesterday is not automatically equivalent to a delivery date agreed with a customer. Both may matter, but changing them has different consequences.

## Name one outcome for the remaining time

Choose a result you can observe within the current planning period. “Grow the business” leaves too much room for interpretation. “Prepare and discuss two proposals with suitable prospects” describes work you can complete and evidence you can review.

Use an outcome within your control. You can send a proposal and arrange a conversation; you cannot decide on someone else's behalf that they will buy. Keep the commercial aim visible without turning it into a promise your calendar cannot deliver.

Ask what currently prevents progress toward that outcome. If prospects cannot understand the service, a clearer offer may deserve attention. If nobody relevant sees the offer, rewriting the headline for the tenth time may leave the discovery problem untouched.

## Estimate capacity from the week you actually have

Open the calendar. Subtract commitments, meetings, administration and time away from work. Include preparation and recovery where they affect your ability to do the task.

Estimate tasks as complete pieces of work. A two-hour article draft may also need fact checking, editing, artwork and publication. A fifteen-minute meeting may require a useful hour of preparation.

Leave a buffer that reflects your circumstances. There is no universal percentage that fits every business. If interruptions are frequent or estimates are unfamiliar, reserve more room and choose a smaller plan.

Use rough ranges when needed. “Two to four hours” makes uncertainty visible. If the plan only fits the optimistic end of every range, revise it before the week begins.

## Compare tasks without manufacturing precision

For each optional task, answer four questions:

| Question | What a useful answer looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Connection | Which part of the current outcome does it help? |
| Evidence | What have customers, usage or previous attempts shown? |
| Dependency | What must happen before this can produce useful work? |
| Effort | What does finishing it require, including review? |

[Intercom's RICE framework](https://www.intercom.com/blog/rice-simple-prioritization-for-product-managers/) compares reach, impact, confidence and effort for product decisions. It can be useful when those estimates have a meaningful basis. For a solo business choosing between unlike tasks, a short explanation may be more honest than a precise numerical score.

For example, a prospect who requested a proposal is different evidence from your belief that a new social platform might be promising. Neither guarantees a sale. The distinction helps you decide whether the next step should be delivery, follow-up or a small investigation.

Do not automatically reject uncertain work. If an important assumption remains untested, choose the smallest useful task that could reveal more about it. Our guide to [prototyping the biggest risk](https://coachrank.lol/blog/prototype-the-risk-before-the-product) explains how to match the experiment to the question.

## A worked example: six hours and five competing tasks

Consider a fictional independent designer. After client delivery and administration, they have eight hours available this week. They reserve two hours for spillover, leaving six hours to pursue one outcome: prepare a clearer offer for two warm prospects.

Their optional tasks look like this:

| Task | Estimated time | Reason to consider it | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Finish the requested proposal | 2 hours | One prospect is waiting for a defined scope | Schedule first |
| Add an annotated sample project | 2 hours | Both prospects asked what the deliverable looks like | Schedule second |
| Follow up with the two prospects | 1 hour | They have already discussed the service | Schedule after the sample is ready |
| Refresh the logo | 3 hours | The designer prefers a different style | Defer; no current buyer evidence |
| Start a new social channel | 4 hours | It might create future awareness | Revisit after choosing a discovery experiment |

The selected work uses five of the six planned hours. The remaining hour can absorb an estimate error or cover final checks. The separately reserved two hours remain available for spillover from the rest of the week.

Notice the dependency: the follow-up should include the sample, so it happens after the sample is complete. A ranked list that ignores that relationship would be harder to execute.

This example does not mean warm prospects should always take priority over marketing. If there were no relevant conversations, a well-chosen discovery task might be the better use of time. The current situation determines the decision.

## Give deferred tasks a condition for returning

A deferred list becomes useful when it explains why work is waiting. “Later” keeps the task available to interrupt you every day.

Write a condition such as “Revisit after the two prospect conversations, using what we learn about the offer.” For a website change, the condition might be a repeated usability problem. For a new channel, it might be enough capacity to run and evaluate a bounded experiment.

Some tasks should be closed rather than deferred. If you no longer need the result, preserve useful material and remove the implied commitment. For a larger backlog, use the [unfinished-project audit](https://coachrank.lol/blog/unfinished-project-audit).

## Turn the choices into starting instructions

Each scheduled task needs a first action and a finish condition. “Work on proposal” leaves room to wander. “Draft the deliverables, exclusions and review points, then send the proposal after checking the numbers” gives the session a direction.

Put the work into real calendar space. If a task requires a continuous two-hour block, do not assume four scattered half hours will serve equally well. If the necessary time is unavailable, narrow the task or change the plan.

When new work arrives, ask what it would replace. If it is a real emergency, adjust the week and communicate the consequences. If it is an interesting possibility, capture it for review instead of silently adding it to a full plan.
