The article is nearly finished. Then a new opening appears. The new opening changes the middle. The new middle suggests a different example. By evening, the piece has become a more sophisticated draft of a different assignment.

Some revisions improve the work. Others reopen a decision that was never clearly made. Without a release standard, “better” can mean changing the project indefinitely.

[GOV.UK's description of the alpha phase](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/agile-delivery/how-the-alpha-phase-works) ties completion to having enough evidence for a next-stage decision. Creative projects can also benefit from a stage-specific finish: what must this version establish or deliver before it can leave the studio?

## Define the version you are finishing

Write a short release sentence. For example: “This article will help a first-time manager prepare one useful delegation conversation, using a concrete example and a short checklist.”

That sentence leaves room for many creative choices. It also makes it possible to ask whether a proposed addition serves this version. A history of management theory may be interesting without being necessary to the current assignment.

Name the intended format and scope. A short guide, a campaign concept and a finished production asset require different levels of completeness. Avoid judging a concept sketch as if it were a launch-ready product, or releasing a sketch under a finished promise.

## Establish a small set of non-negotiable checks

Choose checks that reflect the consequence of releasing the work. For an article, they might include factual support, a coherent argument, useful examples, readable structure and functioning links. For a paid product, access, essential functionality and accurate purchase terms also matter.

Make each check inspectable. “The piece feels powerful” may guide an editorial conversation, but it cannot replace checking whether the central claim is supported.

A release standard is not permission to ignore material defects. It is a way to decide what needs attention now without treating every possible improvement as equally urgent.

## Sort revision notes into three groups

During the final review, label each note before acting on it.

| Group | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Required for this version | A material error, missing step or problem with the agreed task. |
| Useful in a later version | An improvement that does not prevent this version doing its job. |
| A different direction | A change to the audience, purpose, format or underlying concept. |

The labels are decisions, not natural facts. Discuss disagreements with the person responsible for release. A seemingly small addition can become essential if it resolves a dangerous misunderstanding; a large new section can remain optional if it serves another project.

Keep later ideas somewhere retrievable. Capturing them reduces the temptation to insert everything into the current version simply because you do not want to lose it.

## Review in the form the audience will encounter

A draft in an editor can hide problems that appear in the final format. Read the article on a phone. Open the exported proposal. Play the video with the intended captions. Follow the product's actual next step.

Ask someone appropriate to the audience to attempt the task where practical. Their observation can reveal a problem that another private polishing session would miss.

Do not treat one person's taste as a final verdict. Focus on what they understood, where they became uncertain and whether the work supports its purpose. Keep aesthetic judgment alongside those observations rather than pretending either can replace the other.

## Make release a decision with an owner

At the agreed checkpoint, review the required fixes and decide whether the version is ready. If it is not, name the remaining issue and a next review point. Avoid “one more pass” without a question for that pass to answer.

After release, collect response relevant to the assignment. A thoughtful reply may teach more about an article's usefulness than a large impression count. A recurring support question may reveal a missing instruction in a product.

Finishing does not mean declaring the work incapable of improvement. It means delivering a version whose purpose and limits you can stand behind. That decision lets the work meet the people it was made for, where the next useful lesson can finally begin.
