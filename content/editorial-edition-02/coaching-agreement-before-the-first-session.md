Two people can enter a coaching engagement with good intentions and different ideas about what has been purchased. One expects a reflective conversation. The other expects expert advice and a written plan. An employer may expect updates neither has discussed.

Those differences should become visible before the work depends on them. Clarity about the relationship is part of the service, not a formality to rush through on the way to the “real” coaching.

The [ICF Code of Ethics](https://coachingfederation.org/ethics/code-of-ethics/) addresses agreements, confidentiality and professional responsibilities. The questions below are an operational conversation guide. They do not replace a suitable written agreement or advice about obligations in your circumstances.

## Explain what kind of help is being offered

Ask the coach to describe how sessions work in plain language. What role will questioning, reflection, information and suggested exercises play? If the coach also consults or mentors, how will they make a change of role explicit?

The client should be able to explain the service back without relying on professional vocabulary. “We will work on the decisions and behavior involved in my transition to management” is more informative than “we will unlock transformation.”

Also discuss what the service does not cover. A clear boundary helps the client recognize when another form of support or expertise is more appropriate. The coach should not imply qualifications or capabilities they do not hold.

## Agree on a direction without promising a predetermined outcome

Explore what the client wants to make more understandable or do differently. A broad direction can be useful at the start, provided there is room to refine it as the work develops.

Distinguish the client's ambition from a guaranteed result. A coach can describe their process and commitments, but commercial outcomes, promotions and other people's behavior involve factors outside the engagement.

A fictional client might want to handle delegation more effectively. An initial focus could be examining a recent handover and planning a different approach. That gives the first session a practical starting point without pretending the entire development journey is already known.

## Discuss the information boundaries explicitly

Ask how notes are kept, who can access them, whether sessions may be recorded and how any proposed digital tools would be used. Discuss confidentiality and its limits using the actual agreement and applicable professional obligations.

If an employer or another sponsor pays, clarify the reporting arrangement before sharing substantive material. What may be reported about attendance, broad goals or progress? What remains private? How will any change be agreed?

Do not assume that payment automatically gives a sponsor access to the content of conversations. Equally, do not promise absolute secrecy without understanding the relevant limits. The useful outcome is a shared and accurate understanding, not a reassuring slogan.

## Make the practical exchange easy to understand

Use a simple pre-engagement checklist:

| Area | Question to resolve |
| --- | --- |
| Sessions | How often, how long and in what format? |
| Preparation | What is expected before a session? |
| Between-session contact | What support is included, and how quickly can a reply be expected? |
| Changes | How are cancellations, rescheduling or changed needs handled? |
| Payment | What is included in the fee and when is payment due? |
| Concerns | How can either person raise an issue or end the engagement? |

These details influence the experience. A client expecting unlimited messaging may feel let down by a service designed around scheduled sessions, even when both parties initially liked one another.

## Revisit the agreement when the work changes

Clarity is not finished after a document is signed. A new sponsor, a different goal or an emerging conflict may require another conversation.

Build in an opportunity to review the relationship. Ask what is useful, what feels unclear and whether the current arrangement still fits. Make it possible for the client to disagree or request a change without being treated as resistant to growth.

A thoughtful beginning creates room for more honest work later. Before the first substantive session, make sure both people can describe the purpose, the boundaries and the next step in language they understand.
