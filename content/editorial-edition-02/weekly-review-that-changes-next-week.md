A weekly review can become a ceremony of moving overdue tasks into the future. The list looks current. The underlying commitments remain impossible. By Wednesday, the same items are overdue again.

The useful output of a review is a changed decision. Something is scheduled, simplified, delegated, declined or consciously left for later. Tidying the list can support that work, but it should not substitute for it.

David Allen's [Getting Things Done materials](https://gettingthingsdone.com/2015/07/podcast-07-guided-gtd-weekly-review/) present a recurring review as part of keeping a trusted system current. The compact review below is our own working format for founders and independent professionals.

## Look backward for evidence, not a verdict

Open your calendar, project notes and a few completed artifacts. What did you actually finish? Which commitments expanded? Where did the plan meet a responsibility you had not accounted for?

Avoid turning this into a weekly evaluation of your worth. A missed task can reflect unclear scope, missing information, a changed priority or a genuinely difficult week. The explanation matters because it determines the next decision.

Write one observation you want to carry forward. “The proposal required client data we did not have” is useful. “I need more discipline” gives you much less to work with.

## Reconcile the open commitments

Collect the promises that are spread across messages, notes and memory. Identify the ones you have accepted, the ones you are waiting on and the ones that merely sounded interesting.

For each active item, ask what the next visible action is. “Improve our brand” is not ready for the calendar. “Draft a plain description of the workshop offer” is something you can begin.

Then ask whether the commitment still belongs to you. If someone else owns it, record the handover and the next check. If it is no longer needed, close it rather than preserving it indefinitely as a symbol of ambition.

## Inspect the week before adding the work

Put fixed responsibilities on the page first: delivery, travel, care, appointments and necessary administration. Then look at the usable space that remains.

A fictional consultant may have two delivery days, a half-day of preparation and several existing conversations. Planning five days of new business development on top of that is not a motivational strategy. It is a capacity error.

Use this small table to make the tradeoffs visible:

| Commitment | Next action | Space needed | Decision |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Client delivery | Prepare the agreed workshop | Protected preparation block | Keep. |
| Offer revision | Draft the first section | One modest writing session | Narrow. |
| New side project | Define the initial question | No realistic space this week | Defer explicitly. |

Estimate imperfectly, then learn from the difference. The purpose is a credible plan, not an exact prediction of every hour.

## Choose what will not happen

A review becomes honest when it names the work that cannot fit. If another person depends on it, communicate the change. Moving a private due date does not renegotiate an external promise.

Explain the relevant choice plainly: what you can deliver, what would need to move and when a revised commitment is realistic. Avoid waiting until the deadline passes to reveal a conflict that is already visible.

Keep deferred ideas in a separate place with a reason or a revisit point. They should not compete with active commitments every time you open the day's list.

## Leave a short plan and a useful question

Finish with a few specific actions, the space to attempt them and one question to review next time. Perhaps you want to learn whether a smaller writing session is easier to protect or whether an earlier client request reduces preparation delays.

Do not expand the review until maintaining it becomes another substantial project. If the week is unusually difficult, a brief check of urgent commitments and necessary renegotiations may be the appropriate version.

A good review makes your responsibilities more understandable and your next actions more realistic. It does not need to make the week look impressive. It needs to help the week work.
