The session is nearly over. The coach offers a sensible next step. The client nods. Both leave with the feeling that a plan exists.

At the next session, the action has not happened. It is tempting to treat this as an accountability problem. First, consider whether the action was ever a real choice the client understood and wanted to make.

The [ICF competencies](https://coachingfederation.org/credentialing/coaching-competencies/) include supporting client growth through partnership. In practice, the closing minutes deserve the same attention to the client's meaning as the conversation itself. An action list should not become the coach's proof that the session was useful.

## Ask what the client wants to carry forward

Begin with what changed in their understanding. What feels clearer? What remains unresolved? Which part of the conversation matters outside the session?

Do not assume the answer must be an immediate task. The client may need to observe a situation, gather information or sit with a new distinction before deciding. Those can be legitimate next steps if the person understands their purpose.

If action would help, invite the client to propose it. Their first idea may need clarification, but it gives you a starting point grounded in their priorities rather than your preferred solution.

## Move from intention to a recognizable situation

“I will communicate better” does not tell the client what to do when the working day begins. Explore a situation where the intention matters.

A fictional manager might choose to make the next project handover clearer. Ask what that means in practice: which handover, with whom, and what would they do differently? They might decide to describe the expected result and invite the colleague to identify uncertainties.

Avoid designing the entire conversation for them. The purpose is enough specificity to make the action usable, while preserving room for their judgment in the actual situation.

## Explore friction without predicting failure

Ask what could make the action difficult and what support would be useful. This is an opportunity to improve the plan, not to demand that the client promise to overcome every obstacle.

Use these prompts selectively:

| Planning question | What it can clarify |
| --- | --- |
| When might this situation occur? | Whether the action has a realistic opportunity. |
| What will you need beforehand? | Information, preparation or agreement. |
| What might get in the way? | A practical obstacle or competing responsibility. |
| What would a smaller useful attempt be? | An option if the original plan is too large. |
| How would you like to review it? | The form of support the client actually wants. |

Do not use the table as a required interrogation. Follow what the client needs to make the choice their own.

## Agree on evidence that serves the learning

A review does not need to establish that the person “succeeded” in a broad sense. It can examine whether the action happened, what they noticed and what they learned from the response.

For the handover example, useful evidence might include the wording the manager used, questions the colleague asked and what remained unclear. A self-rating can supplement that record, but it should not replace the situation itself.

Be careful about collecting material involving other people. The client can discuss their own learning without bringing identifiable employee messages or confidential documents into a session unnecessarily.

## Make the next review a conversation

If the action does not happen, explore the difference between the plan and the conditions. Was the opportunity absent? Was the task too large? Did the client decide it no longer fit? Was there an unspoken concern?

A missed attempt can reveal information. Treating every missed action as resistance can obscure that information and make the client less willing to describe what actually happened.

If the action does happen, examine what it taught rather than stopping at congratulations. The response may suggest a revision, another experiment or a different question.

Before ending a session, ask the client to describe the next step in their own words and say whether it feels like their choice. A smaller action that belongs to them has a clearer place in the work than an impressive plan accepted mainly to satisfy the room.
