You ask a thoughtful question. While the other person answers, you begin preparing the next thoughtful question. By the time they finish, you have an elegant response ready and only a partial understanding of what they said.

The conversation can sound like coaching while functioning like a prepared interview. The questions are good in isolation; the connection between them is weak.

The [ICF coaching competencies](https://coachingfederation.org/credentialing/coaching-competencies/) include active listening and responsiveness to the client. A useful everyday discipline follows from that emphasis: let the answer influence the next move.

## Notice the difference between words and your interpretation

A client says, “I keep stepping in when my team gets stuck.” You may hear a need to delegate. They may be describing unclear responsibilities, missing training, an urgent operational problem or something else entirely.

Begin by checking the meaning. “When you say you step in, what happens in a recent example?” gives the person room to define the situation. “Why don't you trust your team?” installs an interpretation before it has been examined.

Keep your expertise available without allowing it to overwrite the account. A familiar pattern can suggest a useful question, but it should not become a diagnosis of the person from a single sentence.

## Reflect tentatively and leave room for correction

A reflection can be short: “It sounds as though the difficult moment is deciding when to help and when to wait. Have I understood that?”

The invitation to correct you matters. If the person says no, follow the correction instead of defending the interpretation. You are checking shared meaning, not demonstrating that you can summarize perfectly.

Avoid repeating every sentence mechanically. Reflection is useful when it clarifies a relevant distinction or checks an uncertain understanding. Too much repetition can interrupt the person's thinking just as readily as too many new questions.

## Follow a thread that the person recognizes

Consider this fictional exchange:

| The person says | A possible next move |
| --- | --- |
| “I always end up rewriting the work.” | Ask for a recent instance. |
| “The result was different from what I expected.” | Explore how the expected result had been described. |
| “I thought it was obvious.” | Ask what the colleague could actually see or refer to. |
| “I never showed an example.” | Ask what, if anything, that changes in the person's understanding. |

This is not a script to reproduce. The next move depends on the answer. The person may reveal a completely different issue at any point, and a useful conversation should be able to follow it.

## Give thinking room without turning silence into a technique

A pause can allow someone to continue, reconsider or find a more accurate word. It can also be ambiguous. They may be thinking, unsure of the question or waiting for you to clarify.

Do not treat a particular number of silent seconds as a universal standard. Attend to the person and the context. You can ask whether they would like time, a different question or a summary of where the conversation has reached.

The aim is not to create a dramatic coaching moment. It is to make space usable for the person doing the thinking. Cultural expectations, communication preferences and accessibility needs can all shape what feels useful.

## Notice when you are carrying a preferred answer

Before asking the next question, privately check whether you are willing to hear something unexpected. If you are trying to steer toward your own solution, consider making the shift explicit: “I have an observation from my experience. Would it be useful to hear it?”

In a workplace conversation, also distinguish a fixed requirement from an open decision. If a deadline cannot change, do not ask questions that imply it can. Explain the boundary and explore the choices that remain.

End by asking what the person now understands differently and what they want to do with that understanding. Their answer may be smaller or less dramatic than the one you imagined. It should still be theirs.

Good listening is visible in the direction a conversation takes. The strongest next question may be the one you could not have prepared before hearing the answer.
