You know the slides. You can recite the opening. Then someone asks a question you did not expect, and the presentation suddenly feels like a different task.

That is a clue about the rehearsal. Repeating the smooth version prepares the smooth version. A more useful session also explores the places where the audience, technology or available information may change the route.

The [GOV.UK guidance on prototypes](https://www.gov.uk/service-manual/design/making-prototypes) treats a prototype as a way to explore an idea before committing to the finished service. We can borrow that practical attitude for a presentation: build a small rehearsal around an uncertainty. This is an editorial exercise, not a claim that one routine reliably removes performance anxiety.

## Give the audience a task

Before practicing delivery, write what the audience should be able to do afterward. A founder may want a prospect to decide whether a pilot fits. A coach may want a workshop participant to attempt one new conversation. A creative director may need approval for a direction and its constraints.

That task tells you what belongs in the presentation. It also gives a rehearsal partner something to inspect beyond whether you sound polished. Could they explain the decision? Did they understand what they were being asked to approve? Which necessary detail was missing?

If your only objective is “make a strong impression,” almost any criticism can send you into another cycle of cosmetic editing.

## Rehearse five transitions separately

Try these short scenes before doing a full run. Keep each attempt small enough to repeat.

| Transition | A useful rehearsal question |
| --- | --- |
| Into the room | Can I explain the purpose without a long preamble? |
| Between ideas | Does the next section follow from the previous one? |
| Into a question | Can I check what the person needs before answering? |
| Out of uncertainty | Can I state what I do not know and a way to find out? |
| Into the close | Is the requested next step specific and appropriate? |

Choose the transition that is most consequential for this audience. A procurement discussion and a public creative talk will not need the same practice.

## Practice an honest response to an unknown

Imagine a fictional consultant presenting a research service. A prospect asks for a result the consultant cannot guarantee. A hurried answer might become an accidental promise. A more useful response separates the service from the outcome: “We can deliver the interviews and synthesis described here. I cannot promise a particular sales increase. Which decision would you need the research to support?”

Rehearse that distinction with your own work. Identify the limits before the live conversation. If a question requires verification, state who will check, what they will check and when an answer can reasonably follow.

You do not need a clever response to every possible objection. You need a reliable way to clarify, answer within your knowledge and preserve a useful next step.

## Add friction without making the rehearsal hostile

Ask a trusted partner to introduce one realistic variation: a shorter time slot, a request for an example, or a question about scope. Agree on the variation in advance if surprise would make the exercise less useful.

Record the behavior you want to inspect. For instance, did you repeat the question accurately? Did the answer address it? Did you return to the main thread without rushing through the remaining material?

If you record the rehearsal, obtain consent and keep the recording private. Review a short segment rather than judging your entire personality on camera. One observable adjustment gives the next attempt a purpose.

## Finish with a recovery card

Write a small card containing the audience task, three essential points, an honest way to handle unknowns and the closing request. It is a navigation aid, not a second script to memorize.

Then run the presentation once in conditions close to the real setting. Check the practical details: visibility, sound, links and the file you will actually use. Keep backup material proportionate to the event.

Excellent communication includes what happens when the conversation becomes real. Practice the transitions, and your preparation can support that conversation instead of asking the audience to cooperate with a perfect script.
