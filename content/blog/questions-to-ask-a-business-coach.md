Most first calls with a coach are structured as a sales conversation with a discovery layer on top. That is reasonable — they are running a business. But it means the questions flow one way unless you bring your own.

These nine are ordered roughly by how much they reveal per minute spent.

## On fit

**1. "Who do you work with most often?"**
You are listening for a specific population, not a flattering description of you. Vagueness here usually means they take whoever calls, which is fine but tells you the process is not built for your situation.

**2. "What kind of client does badly with you?"**
The best question on this list. Anyone who has coached for a while knows exactly who they cannot help. An answer means self-knowledge and a real practice. "Anyone can benefit" means either inexperience or a sales script.

**3. "What would you want me to bring to each session?"**
Reveals whether there is a method or just a conversation. Both can work, but you should know which you are buying.

## On the work

**4. "What does month three look like, compared to month one?"**
You are testing whether they think in arcs or in appointments. A coach who cannot describe how the work changes over time is selling you a recurring meeting.

**5. "When have you told a client to stop working with you?"**
Follows naturally from question two and confirms it. Someone who has ended engagements on their own initiative is not optimising purely for retention.

**6. "What do you do when I am not doing the thing I said I would do?"**
This is the actual job. Accountability is where coaching either works or quietly becomes therapy without the training for it. Listen for a real mechanism, not a philosophy.

## On terms

**7. "What exactly is included between sessions?"**
"Access" ranges from same-day replies to a shared inbox nobody reads. Get it specific enough to be checkable.

**8. "What is the notice period, and what happens if it is not working?"**
Covered in more depth in our piece on coaching costs, but worth repeating: a clear, unembarrassed answer here predicts a clear answer to everything else.

**9. "What do you not do?"**
Coaching sits next to therapy, consulting and advice that requires a licence. A coach who knows their boundary — and will say so — is a professional. A coach who claims a broad remit across financial, medical or legal territory is a risk, whatever their intentions.

## What you are really testing

Every one of these is a proxy for the same thing: whether this person is comfortable being precise about their own work.

Coaching is a field with no protected title and enormous variance in practice. Precision is the most reliable available signal, and it shows up in how someone answers boring commercial questions long before it shows up in results.
