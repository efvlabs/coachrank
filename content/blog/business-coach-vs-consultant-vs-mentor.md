These three get used interchangeably, usually by people selling one of them. They are genuinely different, and the difference is not seniority or price. It is **who owns the decision, and who does the work.**

## The short version

**A consultant does the work.** You have a problem with a knowable answer — a pricing model, a market entry, a restructure. They bring expertise you do not have, produce something, and leave. The output is a deliverable. You own the result; they owned the analysis.

**A coach helps you do the work.** You have a decision only you can make, or a pattern you keep repeating. They bring process, questions and accountability rather than answers. The output is a changed decision or a changed behaviour. You own both the thinking and the result.

**A mentor has been where you are.** Usually informal, often unpaid, and specific to a path — someone ten years ahead in your industry who tells you what they would do. The output is judgement borrowed from experience. The relationship is personal and rarely contractual.

## How to tell which one you need

Ask what would count as success in three months.

If the answer is *a thing exists that does not exist now* — a plan, a model, a system, a hire — you probably want a consultant. Coaching will feel slow and evasive, because a coach will keep handing the question back to you when what you wanted was the answer.

If the answer is *I am making a different decision, or handling something differently* — you have stopped avoiding a conversation, you have fired someone you should have fired last year, you are no longer the bottleneck — that is coaching. A consultant will produce a document and you will not change.

If the answer is *I want to know how someone else navigated this* — that is mentorship, and paying for it usually makes it worse. The value in mentorship is that it is freely given by someone with no commercial interest in your continuation.

## The overlap, honestly

Plenty of good practitioners do more than one. A business coach who has run three companies will occasionally answer directly instead of asking, and should. A consultant with a long engagement inevitably coaches the team.

The label matters less than the contract. What you want to avoid is paying coaching rates for consulting hours — long engagements where nothing is delivered and nothing changes, because neither party ever agreed which mode they were in.

## The question that resolves it

Before you hire, ask: **"At the end of this, what will exist that does not exist now?"**

A consultant will name an artefact. A coach will name a change in you or your decisions. If the answer is vague in a way that could mean either, you have found the real risk in the engagement, and it is worth resolving before money changes hands rather than after.
