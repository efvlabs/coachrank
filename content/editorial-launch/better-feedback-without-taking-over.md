Someone brings you a difficult situation. Before they have finished describing it, you can see a possible answer. You offer it, explain why it works, and leave the conversation feeling helpful.

The other person leaves with your plan. It is less clear whether they understand the problem more deeply, agree with the plan, or know how to adapt it when circumstances change.

Advice can be valuable. So can a conversation that helps someone examine their own thinking. The skill is to understand what kind of help is needed and to make that choice visible.

## Agree on what the conversation is for

Before exploring solutions, ask what would make the conversation useful. The person may want a decision, a fresh perspective, a careful hearing or your specific expertise.

For a manager, there may also be an organizational responsibility that cannot be negotiated away. If a safety requirement, deadline or performance standard is fixed, say so. Presenting a predetermined answer as an open coaching conversation makes it harder to trust the questions that follow.

Professional coaching has its own standards and boundaries. The [International Coaching Federation’s core competencies](https://coachingfederation.org/credentialing/coaching-competencies/) describe areas including establishing agreements, listening, supporting awareness and facilitating growth. This article uses those broad ideas as context for an everyday working conversation. Its example questions are original suggestions, not an ICF assessment or credentialing framework.

## Stay close to a specific event

“I am terrible at delegation” is a large conclusion. A recent example gives you something more useful to explore.

Ask the person to describe a task they delegated: what they asked for, what the other person understood, when they checked in and what happened. Notice which parts are observable and which are interpretations.

You might discover that the task’s outcome was clear but the authority to make decisions was not. Or that the manager intervened before the agreed review point. Or that the person receiving the task lacked information the manager assumed was obvious.

These are illustrative possibilities. The purpose of the conversation is to discover what happened in this case, rather than fitting someone’s experience into your favorite explanation.

## Offer observations without turning them into verdicts

A useful observation gives the other person room to examine it. “You do not trust your team” may close the conversation. “You mentioned checking the draft three times before the review you agreed” refers to something they can confirm, correct or explain.

Follow the observation with an open question. What were they concerned would happen? What information did they have at the time? What would they want to repeat or change?

Do not use a question mark to disguise an instruction. “Don’t you think you should trust them more?” already contains the preferred answer. If you want to offer advice, own it as advice.

## Ask before changing roles

There are moments when your experience is relevant. A person who is learning a new process may need information before they can make a sensible decision.

You can say, “I have an example from similar work. Would it help to hear it?” Explain the context and limits of the example, then ask what seems applicable. Make space for the possibility that your previous solution does not fit this situation.

In a professional relationship, this distinction belongs in the broader agreement about what you provide. Coaching, mentoring, training and consulting may overlap in practice, but the buyer should understand which kind of help they are receiving.

## Help the person design a next step they own

An insight becomes more useful when it changes a decision or experiment. Invite the person to choose a small action, identify the support it requires and decide how they will judge what happened.

For the delegation example, they might choose to write an outcome, a decision boundary and a review point before the next handover. The next conversation can examine how that changed the experience.

The following structure is a CoachRank working guide, not a script to apply mechanically:

| Stage | A question you could ask |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | What would make this conversation useful today? |
| Specifics | Can we look at the most recent time this happened? |
| Perspective | What are you treating as certain, and what is still an assumption? |
| Choice | What options do you see, including leaving things as they are? |
| Action | What is one step you are willing and able to take? |
| Review | What would help you judge whether that step was useful? |

Listen to the answers. Moving through a list of questions without following the person’s meaning can become its own form of taking over.

## Respect the limits of the relationship

A colleague does not automatically consent to a deep personal exploration because you have learned a coaching technique. Ask permission before moving into sensitive territory, and respect a decision not to discuss it.

Keep the conversation within your role and competence. Where someone needs specialized support, help them consider an appropriate next step instead of presenting ordinary workplace coaching as a solution to every problem.

Be clear about confidentiality, especially when you are both a manager and a sounding board. Explain what you can keep private and what your responsibilities require you to act on. Trust depends on that clarity as much as on the warmth of the conversation.

## Review the quality of your help

Afterward, ask yourself what the person understood or chose more clearly. Did you learn something that changed your initial view? Did they leave with an action they own, or mainly with a task you assigned?

The answer does not have to flatter you. A conversation can be useful because it reveals that advice, training or a direct management decision is needed.
